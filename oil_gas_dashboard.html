The industry · 2024–25 baseline

IOverview

Together, oil and natural gas supply well over half the world's primary energy, oil about 31%, gas about 25%. Both are strikingly concentrated in a few states, and both are, unusually for industries of their scale, mostly owned by governments rather than by companies you can buy shares in. Baseline figures are 2024–25; the market snapshots later in this dashboard are dated to July 2026.

State-owned producers hold the great majority of what is in the ground. Their reserve lives run to decades, up to 99 years at current rates for the largest Gulf holders, which is why they set the pace of the market rather than responding to it. Saudi Aramco alone reports around 250 billion barrels of oil equivalent, more than ten times ExxonMobil.

IIHow we got here

Today's market cannot be read without the institution that made it. Before 1960 seven Western companies, the "Seven Sisters", controlled over 85% of world reserves and simply posted the price of crude. OPEC was founded in Baghdad in September 1960, days after those companies cut the posted price a second time, and the following sixty years are the story of pricing power moving from the companies to the producing states, then being contested by the market itself. Prices below are real, in constant 2025 dollars.

PRE-1960
Seven Sisters

Seven Western majors control 85%+ of reserves and post the price. Producing states are royalty collectors, not price-setters.

1960–1972
OPEC forms

Five founders organise in Baghdad to halt price cuts. For a decade OPEC holds the line but real prices keep drifting down.

1973–1985
Producer power

The oil weapon, nationalisations and two shocks hand OPEC control. Real prices hit their all-time high, then over-reach.

1986–NOW
Market era

Benchmarks (Brent, WTI) and futures set the price; OPEC influences but no longer dictates. OPEC+ (2016) adds Russia to the lever.

The oil order — a timeline

The institutional history, from OPEC's founding to the present crisis. Blue marks the shifts in who holds the price; amber-red marks the shocks that forced them.

1960
OPEC is founded
Five states meet in Baghdad after the majors cut posted prices twice. A budget of £150,000 and no immediate power, the Seven Sisters simply ignore it, but the producers now have a single voice.
1965 · 1968
Institutions take shape
The Secretariat moves to Vienna. Libya, the UAE (as Abu Dhabi), Algeria and others join through the decade, broadening the bloc beyond the Gulf founders.
1973–74
The oil weapon
Arab members embargo the West after the Yom Kippur War; prices roughly quadruple. Pricing power passes decisively from the companies to the states, and stagflation follows across the West.
1970s
Nationalisations
Members take ownership of their reserves from the majors, Saudi Arabia fully nationalising Aramco by 1980. The modern national oil company is born.
1979–80
The Iranian revolution
Iran's output collapses; the second shock drives the real price to its all-time high, ~$125 in today's money.
1986
The counter-shock
High prices spurred North Sea, Alaskan and Mexican supply and killed demand. Prices collapse; OPEC's grip breaks and the market, via Brent and WTI benchmarks, takes over price-setting.
1990 · 1998
War, then glut
Iraq's invasion of Kuwait spikes prices briefly; eight years later the Asian crisis and overproduction sink them to ~$23 real, cheaper than 1946.
2008
The super-spike
Chinese demand and speculation drive WTI to a nominal $147, then the financial crisis halves it within months.
2014–16
The shale challenge
US shale floods the market. OPEC declines to cut, letting prices fall to discipline rivals, a war it does not clearly win.
Dec 2016
OPEC+ is formed
OPEC brings in Russia and nine other producers to manage supply jointly, the most significant institutional change since the 1970s, and the lever still in use today.
2020 · 2022
COVID, then Ukraine
WTI briefly trades below zero as demand vanishes; two years later Russia's invasion sends Brent to $139 and reroutes Russian crude to Asia.
2026 · ongoing
The Hormuz crisis
The US–Israel–Iran conflict closes the strait, the largest supply disruption on record. The UAE quits OPEC in May, the first major departure from the Gulf core. The detailed chronology is at the foot of this dashboard.

IVWhere it goes

Producing a lot is not the same as selling a lot. The US is the top producer of both fuels but consumes most of what it lifts; the great export flows run from the Gulf, Russia and West Africa toward Asia. The single defining fact of the modern oil and gas trade is that almost every large flow now points east.

VWho refines it

Crude is useless until it is refined, and the refining map is different from the production map. The centre of gravity has moved decisively to Asia: China and India built vast new capacity while Europe and, increasingly, the US rationalise ageing plants. Global capacity is roughly 101 mb/d across about 700 refineries.

The mismatch

The production and refining maps no longer line up, and that gap is itself a source of trade. The US exports light shale crude while importing heavy grades its Gulf Coast plants were built to run; Mexico ships heavy crude north and buys back US gasoline. Where oil is pumped, where it is processed, and where it is burned are three different maps, which is exactly why the midstream links between them matter as much as they do.

XGas price, three markets, not one

The single most important fact about natural gas: unlike oil, it has no world price. Pipeline dependence and the cost of liquefaction split it into three regional markets, Henry Hub (US), TTF (Europe) and JKM (Asian spot LNG). The 2026 Hormuz crisis has pulled them violently apart, because Qatar , ~20% of world LNG, ships almost entirely through the strait. Spot levels, week of 13–17 July 2026.

HH–TTF spread

Gas is the mirror image of the oil story, sharper. Where the oil market is global and a Gulf disruption lifts one world price, the gas market is regional: the same shock barely touches US Henry Hub but sends European and Asian prices to multi-year highs. Qatar declared force majeure on its LNG early in the conflict, and with European storage entering the crisis below the five-year average, the burden falls on Europe and price-sensitive South Asian buyers, the demand that gets destroyed first.

XIVHow it happened

The market has now priced this conflict three separate times: the shock, the peace, and the relapse.

28 February
War begins. The Strait of Hormuz effectively closes as the IRGC warns vessels off, mines the channel and attacks merchant shipping. Brent ~$70.
March
The largest monthly oil price increase ever recorded. Global supply falls 10.1 mb/d to 97 mb/d, the biggest disruption in the history of the market. Brent crosses $100 on 16 March.
13 April – 29 May
The US blockades Iranian ports. Brent sets a 52-week intraday high of $126.41. Effective spare capacity hits a record low.
18 June
Washington and Tehran sign a memorandum of understanding. Hormuz reopens; tanker traffic picks up sharply. Brent falls below $70 by 1 July.
7 – 15 July
Iran strikes a Qatari LNG tanker near the strait. The US revokes Iran's licence to sell oil and reinstates the naval blockade. Transits fall back below a tenth of baseline.
22 – 23 July
Houthi forces hit two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea, opening a second front. Kazakhstan suspends CPC exports after drone attacks. Brent jumps 7% to $100.65.
24 July
Brent retreats as much as 4.3% as renewed US tariffs weigh on the demand outlook. The 12th consecutive night of US strikes on Iran is completed.
