A wild-harvested tree resin traded for millennia, now a billion-dollar input to perfumery, medicine and incense, with no benchmark price, no production statistics, population collapse documented in its largest source country, and a CITES listing debated and, so far, not made. This page covers the species and grades, the geography of harvest, the science of decline, the trade's structure, and the regulatory argument. No prices are carried as live values.

Grading is by colour and size of the tear, not by species: in Oman the pale, citrus-coloured Hojari tears command the premium, and retail merchants describe Dhofar's resin as the finest in the world.10 Med, merchant sources; there is no published grading standard. Essential oil, resin tears and powder are the three product forms; oil is what the fragrance industry buys, tears what the Gulf and church markets burn.4 5

IIGeography of harvest

A crescent from Dhofar through the Horn of Africa to the Sahel and India.

Dhofar
Fog-fed wadis and coastal cliffs; the Land of Frankincense (Wadi Dawkah, Khor Rori, Shisr, al-Balid) inscribed by UNESCO in 2000 High11 12
Sanaag & Bari
Somaliland and Puntland; the East Golis mountains; research conducted under genuine security risk High3 7
Ethiopia
World's largest exporter; Boswellia predicted almost gone within three decades on current trends Med7 8

Oman's own picture is more mixed than the Horn's: a 2025 rapid conservation assessment found complex threat and population patterns; industry suppliers report that severe over-harvesting is limited to specific areas, that large stands go untapped, and that a protected population in Jebel Samhan National Park is regenerating.13 12 Med, the supplier account is an interested party's. Urban expansion is encroaching on trees near Salalah.10

The mechanism is the same everywhere it has been measured: a tree tapped without rest produces seed that will not germinate, and any seedling that does appear is eaten or burned. Resin production can therefore continue for years from an ageing stand that is already ecologically dead.1 14 High

IVStructure of the trade

Clan tenure, local traders, and a handful of fragrance houses.

Harvester
Family or clan holds customary rights to specific trees; taps 8–10 times a season; income shared with goats and other forest products.3 4
Collector / trader
Aggregates and sorts tears by grade in regional towns (Erigavo, Bosaso, Salalah, Mekelle); extends credit; captures the sorting margin.7
Exporter
Ships resin or distils oil; much of Ethiopia's harvest is suggested to be consumed internally rather than exported, which is one reason trade data cannot be tied to harvest volumes.15
Buyer
Fragrance and flavour houses (Firmenich commissioned population surveys in the Golis), pharmaceutical extractors of boswellic acids, church-incense makers, Gulf bakhoor and luban retailers.6 5
There is no exchange, no benchmark and no production statistic. The only quantitative anchors in the public domain are the two estimates in the header, more than 15,000 tonnes a year and up to $1 billion, both from academic and CITES sources and both explicitly approximate.1 2 Oud's market is better documented than this. High
Diamond parallel: frankincense is oud's structural twin, a wild resin from a threatened tree, graded by eye, aggregated by traders, sold to the Gulf and to Western perfumery, with the harvester capturing the least value. The difference is that frankincense has a documented livelihoods base (225,000 people in one region) that makes a trade ban politically and ethically harder than a CITES listing for a Vietnamese plantation species. Listed or not: the case has been made both ways since 2022.

The Plants Committee's earlier warning was that an Appendix II listing would likely be counter-productive to conservation and sustainable use, endangering 225,000 or more livelihoods; the 2025 Edinburgh assessment found it "almost impossible to conclude that international trade is the sole or most significant threat".3 15 Identification of species in traded resin is now achievable by mass spectrometry, removing one technical objection.16 15 High

Compliance reading: a listing would place every Gulf luban and bakhoor importer under the same permit-and-label regime that governs oud. The more likely near-term path is importer-side due diligence, the EU deforestation and forest-product rules being the model, in which case the paperwork lands on the fragrance houses and Gulf distributors rather than on customs.
