Exit depth: who buys when you sell

Every market page carries a Liquidity line and an Exit depth block. This page reads them together: by what route a holding is sold, how often that route is open, what a round trip costs, and who is on the other side when nobody else is. Fifty-one markets, five kinds of door.

IWhat exit depth measures

Liquidity, on the market pages, answers one question: whether a holding can be sold, to whom, at a knowable price. Exit depth breaks that into parts. The route is the mechanism a seller actually uses. The calendar is how often that mechanism is open. The spread is the gap between what a buyer pays on the way in and what a seller receives on the way out, including every fee between. The counterparty is who is bidding when the seller needs to sell, not when the seller would like to.

The four move together but not in step. Fine wine has a price every day and a spread that is small, but the venue trades only between professionals, so a private holder exits through a merchant. 13 A natural pearl strand has a counterparty with very deep pockets and a route that opens perhaps once a decade. 23 A whisky cask has a broker's quote whenever the broker answers the phone, and the broker is often the firm that sold the cask. 18

The pattern that recurs across the register is that depth is a property of the unit, not the sector. A fine troy ounce, a case of a named vintage in bond, a numbered Parmigiano wheel and a slabbed coin against a published bid all have a buyer at a price; a stone, a painting, a share in a stallion and a cask of new-make do not until a buyer appears. Where a market has a standard unit the door is open most days; where every holding is its own unit the door opens when the calendar or the counterparty says so.

Several markets have two doors. Scotch whisky bottles sell at monthly auction, casks through a broker; polished diamonds have a trade network listing 1.7 m stones at asking prices and an auction route for stones over a threshold; wagyu, bluefin and tea have an auction for the product and listed equity for the capital. The register in Section III lists the route a private holder would most plausibly use.

IVWhat a round trip costs

The spread between buying and selling is the cost of the door. On a benchmark it is a dealer's margin; at auction it is two fees and a wait; through a counterparty it is whatever the counterparty decides.

Derived observation

Auction fees are published on the buy side and negotiated on the sell side, so a private holder who both buys and sells at auction pays the full published premium once and an unknown commission once. On a lot that hammers under $1.5m at a major house that is at least 27% before the seller's side is counted, which is why the sector pages for art, cars, watches and whisky bottles all cite five to ten years as the shortest hold that pays. VHow often the door opens

A route that exists is not the same as a route that is open today. The sector pages give the calendar; this is the same information sorted by frequency.

Every trading dayBenchmark

Gold, silver, PGMs, copper, oil, cocoa, coffee; uranium trust units; wine and champagne through a merchant; current Dayi pu-erh in Guangzhou; a dealer's offer on a watch or a handbag.

Weekly to monthlyList and online auction

Heritage coin sales weekly; Parma and Jaén price lists weekly; truffle markets weekly in season; whisky bottles at monthly online auctions; classic cars online.

Twice a yearThe seasons

Geneva jewels in May and November; Old Masters in London in July and December; Keeneland September and November, Tattersalls October and December; art evening sales in spring and autumn.

Once a yearOne department

Sotheby's natural-history sale each July for a mounted dinosaur; the Riyadh falcon auction in August; Uji tencha in May; the annual Habanos festival auction.

Once a decade or lessThe counterparty's appetite

A natural pearl strand; a great stamp rarity; an imperial-jade necklace; a Flightline-grade stallion share at public auction. Between appearances there is no price.

The right-hand columns are where the Scarcity line on a sector page is highest and the Liquidity line lowest. The two are the same fact seen from different sides: an asset that is rarely sold has no running price, and an asset with no running price is held until a buyer appears.

VIThe buyer of last resort

The question that decides a forced sale. Four answers recur across the register, and one of them is nobody.

The benchmark dealer

A bullion bank at the LBMA price, a clearing member on the LME, a fund's authorised participant. The price may be bad on the day, but there is a price and a counterparty obliged by their business model to quote it. Gold, silver, PGMs, copper, oil, cocoa, coffee. 11 12

The trade, through an intermediary

Liv-ex merchants, Greysheet dealers, Parma wholesalers, Antwerp rough traders. A professional market with firm bids, which a private holder reaches only through a member who takes a margin. Wine, champagne, port, coins, cheese, rough and polished diamonds. 13

The one who sold it to you

The cask broker, the attar dealer, the rare-earth dealer offering buy-back at his own quote, the syndicate manager who must approve the buyer, the originating gallery expected to take a living artist's work back. The counterparty exists, sets the price, and may not be solvent when needed. Casks, oud, rare earths, stallion shares, primary-market art. 18 20 21 14

An administered buyer, or nobody

The Québec Federation, which takes delivery at its price and decides whether the syrup ships or goes to reserve; the exporter in Berbera who buys resin for weeks, not years; no one at all for a lab-grown stone. Where the sector page says "Σ5 because there is nothing to hold", this is why. 22 24 25 17

Derived observation

Auction-house guarantees and irrevocable bids put a buyer of last resort under the best lots in a sale, but only those lots, only at the house's discretion, and only in the two seasons when the sale is held. For the holder of an ordinary lot the last-resort buyer at auction is the underbidder, and the sector pages for stamps, fossils and natural pearls each note that this is one or two people. Hyp

VIIIndicators to monitor

  • Buyer's-premium changes at the major houses; a further rise reported for September 2026 is not yet confirmed from the houses' own schedules. Low 4
  • Whether Liv-ex, or any platform, opens direct access to private holders; that would move wine and champagne from the price-list door to the benchmark door.
  • Insolvencies among cask brokers and other buy-back counterparties, which convert a Counterparty door into None for their clients without notice.
  • Any secondary market for lab-grown stones; the online buyers that currently decline the category are the test.
  • The gap between RapNet asking prices and realised dealer bids for polished diamonds, which is the entry cost on that page.
  • The discount of the Sprott uranium trust to its metal, which is what the benchmark door costs when the trust does not redeem.
