Why this exists

The inheritance nobody taught you to read

The largest transfer of wealth between generations is under way, and much of it is not in accounts. It is in cellars, safes, humidors and drawers.

A parent dies and leaves four hundred bottles of Bordeaux. An uncle's cigar collection, forty boxes, some with Cuban seals from before the embargo. A grandmother's jewellery: a sapphire ring with a Burmese certificate, loose diamonds in a paper fold, a strand of pearls. Gold coins. A watch. A painting whose artist you have to look up.

The questions are always the same, and the answers are rarely at hand. What is this? Is it genuine, and can that be proved? How scarce is it, really? Who buys it, where, and at what discount to the number in the catalogue? Does it need to be stored, insured, licensed, declared? Is the market for it rising, falling or disappearing? Would a knowledgeable person hold it, or sell it?

Each of these markets has specialists who know. Almost none has a plain, cited, up-to-date account that a non-specialist can read in twenty minutes and then ask the specialist the right questions. The dealer knows the answers; the heir does not know the questions.

ΣΟΦΙΑ reads thirty-one of these markets with one method, in the same order every time: supply, demand, concentration, price, provenance, liquidity, outlook. Every figure carries its source and date. Each market is rated for how practical it is to hold, from Σ1, where valuation and exit are a phone call, to Σ5, where no sensible route exists. The same page serves the heir deciding what to keep, the collector deciding what to add, and the adviser asked to build a position in something that does not trade on a screen.

It is not advice. It is the briefing you would want before taking any.
