Climate and supply shock

Half the markets covered are grown, fished, tapped or bred, and their supply is set each year by weather, disease and water before any human decision. This page reads the harvest risk recorded on the market pages together: which markets a bad season can reprice, by how much, how fast supply can answer, and why the same shock does opposite things to an agricultural commodity and to a mined one.

IWhat a season can do

For a mined asset supply is a decision: a mine opens, a sight is held, a cartel meets. For a grown one it is a result: rain in Minas Gerais, a cyclone off Madagascar, a dry winter in Khorasan, the number of frost nights in a Québec March. The market pages for the agricultural and living assets all carry a sentence that says so, and this page collects them.

The shock runs in two directions and the sector pages record both. A short crop lifts the price of what exists and, where the asset is held rather than consumed, the holder gains: arabica to a record in October 2025 after drought in Brazil; Uji tencha up 116% in a year; Iranian saffron output down 36% in 2022 on drought. 20 21 22 A good year does the reverse, and the speculative holder who bought the shortage takes the round trip: vanilla from $600 a kilo to $50; cocoa from $12,565 a tonne to $3,000–4,000 inside two years. 23 3

What decides whether a holder can profit from a shock is not the shock but three things the pages also record: whether the asset can be stored through it (beans and bullion can; truffles, oil and fish cannot), how long supply takes to answer (a vine, a tree, a fish, a herd), and whether anyone stands between the producer and the price (a marketing board, a Federation, a futures market). Sections IV and V take those in turn.

Who gained

Holders of futures through the run, and anyone with beans in an exchange warehouse. Not the farmer: marketing boards in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana set farm-gate prices and sell forward, so growers received a fraction of the exchange price while it ran. 6 45

Who paid

Grinders and chocolate makers, who sought beans in Asia and Latin America and raised retail prices; and anyone who bought the shortage in 2024 and held into the 2026 fall. The roll, not the crop, sets the return on any hold beyond one contract. 8 24

What it built

A funding case for cocoa-free chocolate (see Substitutes) and a reason to read the ICCO balance with its revisions attached: the sector page records four published figures for one season's surplus, which is the normal state of agricultural data after a shock.

IVHow fast supply answers

The length of time between a high price and the new supply it calls forth, as the sector pages state it. This is what decides whether a shock is a spike or a regime.

Within a seasonAnnuals and quota

Saffron corms, vanilla (two harvests a year in Uganda), maple taps, bluefin quota decisions. A high price is answered the next year; the shock is a spike. 22 23 38

Two to five yearsHerds, wheels, bushes

Parmigiano supply responds within two years; wagyu is finished at 28–30 months; a tencha field needs about five years before its leaf is matcha-grade, which is why Uji's price tripling has no quick supply answer. 30 39 21

Five to ten yearsTrees and fish

Olive groves yield from year five to seven; date palms from five to seven and fully from ten; black truffle orchards from five to eight; a female sturgeon from eight to ten; a cocoa tree a few years, but a quarter of Côte d'Ivoire's are past their best. 26 32 33 37 4

A decade or moreResin and the wild

Plantation agarwood takes 10–15 years to sinking grade and 15–25 for the premium tier; wild Boswellia and wild sturgeon do not come back on any investable horizon; white truffle has no orchard at all. 35 34 33

NeverThe vintage

A declared port vintage, a champagne year, a Bordeaux harvest: the season is fixed in the bottle and nothing can add to it. Here the shock is the asset, and a bad year for the region is scarcity for the holder. 28 27

VWho absorbs the shock

Between the field and the holder there is usually an institution, and it decides how much of the season reaches the price.

The futures market

Cocoa and coffee pass the shock straight through to anyone holding a contract, in both directions, and charge the roll for the privilege. The price is honest and the holder is fully exposed. 24 20

The marketing board

Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana sell forward and set farm-gate prices, so the grower sees a damped version of the shock and the board carries the difference, which in Ghana meant arrears when it could not fund purchases. 45 6

The Federation and the reserve

Québec's reserve was built to smooth short seasons; it is now empty, and the administered price has to do the work the warehouse was meant to. The nearest thing in agriculture to OPEC, with a warehouse that has run dry. 25

The appellation

The Comité Champagne fixes the marketable yield before each harvest and the Douro's benefício allocates production rights; both convert a season into a regulated quantity, and both are under strain, Port's "in open crisis". 27 28

Nobody

Saffron, vanilla, frankincense and truffles pass the season to the grower and the trader with no buffer; the price can move by multiples and the people who grew it see the least of it. 22 23 34

The vintage itself

Wine, champagne and port store the season in the bottle, and the holder of a good year owns the shock permanently. Here the absorber is the cellar, and the Custody page is the relevant one. 29

VIThree patterns in the record

Pattern 1Round trip

A grown shortage reverses; a mined one persists

Vanilla, cocoa and saffron all went up by multiples and came back within two to four years, because the plant answered. The rough diamond page records the lowest output since 1987 with no major new mines, and copper's refining constraint runs to 2029: when the constraint is geology or capital, the high price stays. The holder of an agricultural shock is holding a spike unless the asset is a vintage.

Watch: replanting and new-field data (cocoa tree age, Uji plantings, olive super-high-density area)

Pattern 2Perishability

If it cannot be stored, the holder cannot own the shock

Saffron, truffles, olive oil, fish, fresh dates and vanilla all lose quality within a season or two. The only way to hold the shock is to hold the farm, the orchard or the producer's equity, which converts a commodity exposure into an operating business with weather risk. The sector pages rate these routes Σ4 and Σ5 for exactly this reason.

Watch: listed-producer results in the season after a shock (Comvita, Deoleo, Ito En, the seafood groups)

Pattern 3Buffer

The institution between field and price is wearing thin

Québec's reserve is empty; Ghana's board ran arrears; the Douro needs a state scheme for unharvested grapes; Champagne's yield mechanism met a 35% index fall anyway. The buffers that made agricultural prices administrable were built for the variance of the twentieth century. If seasons become more variable the shock reaches the holder faster, and the administered-price markets become discovered-price ones.

Watch: reserve levels, board finances and appellation yield decisions
