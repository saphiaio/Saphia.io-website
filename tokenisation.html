Tokenising scarce assets

A token changes how ownership is recorded and transferred. It does not create a price, a buyer or an exit. This page sets out which scarce-asset markets already have what tokenisation needs, and examines natural diamonds as the case where most of it is missing.

IWhat a token changes, and what it does not

A token is a digital record of a claim on an underlying asset, held on a shared ledger. For a physical asset it adds three things: ownership can be split into small units, transferred quickly between approved holders, and tracked without paper. In effect it creates shares in an asset, much as a company issues shares.

Everything that determines whether an investor can get a fair price and get out still sits off-chain: an agreed way to value the asset, a custodian who holds it, a legal right that the token represents, and buyers on the other side. Where those exist, tokenisation can lower costs and widen access. Where they do not, it reproduces an illiquid asset in a faster format.

The evidence so far supports the distinction. A 2025 review of on-chain data found that "most RWA tokens exhibit low trading volumes, long holding periods, and limited investor participation", with "minimal secondary trading" across most asset classes. Med 2 A Bank for International Settlements study of tokenised property found trading rose sharply after local shocks, but the liquidity depended on platforms offering buybacks, which carries insolvency risk for the platform. High 19

A token can
  • Divide one asset into many small units
  • Move title quickly between verified holders
  • Keep a permanent record of transfers
  • Automate distribution of sale proceeds
A token cannot, on its own
  • Set a price where no reference price exists
  • Create buyers or a market maker
  • Prove the asset is what the certificate says
  • Make two non-identical assets interchangeable

Three issuers described live or planned models at the Dubai Diamond Conference. Descriptions are as stated on stage and are not independently verified. Med 14

Model ASingle rare stone

Fractions of one stone

A certified, insured rare stone is held in custody and divided into tokens. Valuation sits with the issuer, which is also the source of the stone.

Open issue: who sets the price at entry and exit

Model BValued at entry

Independent valuation before minting

Stone placed in a separate legal entity, valued independently before tokens are issued; trading only between approved investors. The issuer stated: "Diamonds are not gold. There is no spot price."

Open issue: no revaluation cadence stated

Model CPooled

Pool priced from public data

Value aggregated across a pool of GIA-graded stones, priced from published retail data and disclosed algorithms, so individual grading errors average out.

Open issue: retail prices sit above the wholesale price an investor realises on exit

Three problems still open

Price

No benchmark is set by completed trades. List and platform prices are asking prices 6. A token's value therefore rests on an appraisal, and appraisal methods differ by issuer.

Certificate

The grading report functions as the title document. Conference speakers noted that grading varies between laboratories and over time, and that the same laboratories grade both natural and laboratory-grown stones. 14

Exit

Exits run through redemption to the issuer or a closed investor pool. One law firm cites redemption spreads of 8–15% for gemstone tokens. Low 17

VThe UAE framework

Derived observation

"Redemption at par" is straightforward where par is a gold price or a currency. For a token backed by a diamond, par is an appraisal. The rule therefore moves the valuation problem onto the appraiser and the revaluation schedule, rather than removing it. Hyp

VIIndicators to monitor

  • Publication of any diamond price index built on completed transactions rather than asking prices, and who administers it.
  • VARA approvals of gemstone-backed tokens, and the valuation methods disclosed in their white papers.
  • Secondary-market activity in issued gemstone tokens: holders, transfers and redemptions, against the low turnover found for tokenised assets generally 2.
  • Whether grading laboratories adopt a common standard that separates natural from laboratory-grown stones in a form investors can read.
  • Growth of tokenised gold relative to gold ETFs, as the test case for whether tokens add liquidity where a benchmark already exists 3.
  • Expansion of the Dubai Land Department pilot beyond UAE ID holders, as a signal of how far regulators will open asset tokens to foreign retail investors 9.
