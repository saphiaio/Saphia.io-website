IOrigins of the trade

Fine wine is an export business first and a farming business second.

Bordeaux's commercial identity was set by geography. The port on the Gironde allowed wine to be shipped at scale to England from the Middle Ages, and the light, early-drinking red the English called "claret" became the region's first branded export.1 In the 17th century Dutch engineers drained the Médoc marshes, exposing the gravel banks on which Cabernet Sauvignon now grows; Dutch traders were also among the first négociants.1 2 High

The intermediary structure followed. In 1680 the role of the Bordeaux broker was formalised under Louis XIV through the Compagnie des Courtiers Gourmets-Piqueurs de Vins, standardising trade practice; over the 18th and 19th centuries the courtier–négociant chain expanded with the region's reputation.3 Unlike regions built around small domaines selling locally, Bordeaux entered the modern era with a mature merchant ecosystem and an export culture wired into its identity, which is why, when a ranking was needed in 1855, the Chamber of Commerce could simply ask the brokers.4 High

The recurring pattern across every region in this page: the producer makes the wine, but the market is made by intermediaries, brokers, négociants, merchants, auction houses and, latterly, an exchange. Where those intermediaries are strong (Bordeaux), the market is liquid and the classification is old. Where they were displaced by producer-direct allocation (Burgundy after the 1980s), the market is thin and prices are set by scarcity rather than by trade. Hyp

IIBordeaux

The classification, the Place and en primeur, a 170-year-old system under modern strain.

The 1855 classification

Napoleon III staged the Exposition Universelle in Paris from May to November 1855 and asked for a classification of the Bordeaux wines on display.4 5 The Chamber of Commerce passed the task to the brokers, who ranked châteaux by reputation and trading price into five growths for the reds and delivered the list on 18 April 1855, in under two weeks.5 6 Coverage was confined to the Médoc (Margaux, Saint-Julien, Pauillac, Saint-Estèphe, Haut-Médoc) plus one estate from Graves, Haut-Brion, together with the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac.7 The Right Bank, Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, was not included and operates under different systems or none.8 High

The Place de Bordeaux and en primeur

The Place is the network of courtiers and négociants through which classified growths are distributed. Every transaction from a participating château passes through a courtier, whether at en primeur or years later from the château's own stock; the négociant then sells on to importers and merchants worldwide.2 3 En primeur, the sale of wine in barrel, months after harvest and long before bottling, grew out of this system in the period after 1855, when négociants paid châteaux for wine still in cask, giving the estates cash flow in exchange for a discount.9 Originally a financing mechanism, it became the marketing event of the fine-wine calendar.3 High

The system's weaknesses are structural: it serves the classified estates and leaves smaller properties outside the distribution network, and it depends on the 1855 names, the first growths above all, for its economics.2 When the secondary market trades below release for long enough, the négociants who must take their allocation to preserve access carry the loss; that stress has been the recurring theme of every campaign since the 2010 vintage.

The critic as price-setter

Robert Parker founded The Wine Advocate in 1978, replaced the 20-point scale with a 100-point one, and made his name by calling the 1982 Bordeaux vintage great when contemporaries judged it modest. History vindicated him, and from that point his scores could change the commercial fortunes of estates and regions; he stepped back from the publication in 2012.10 The stylistic drift of both Bordeaux and California toward riper, plusher wines is widely attributed to his influence.11 Med

Bordeaux's share of trade on the Liv-ex exchange fell from more than 70% in the early 2000s to around 35% by 2026 as Burgundy, Champagne and Italy took share.12 The region retains unmatched scale and liquidity, "first among equals" rather than fallen empire, but the en primeur mechanism now only works where the release price is a clear discount to available back vintages.13 12 High

The hierarchy runs régional, village, premier cru, grand cru, and the unit of classification is the climat, a named parcel with registered boundaries, soil, altitude and exposure.15 Ownership is fragmented within it: Clos de Vougeot's 50.6 hectares are divided among more than 80 producers across over 100 parcels.15 The classification therefore tells a buyer where the grapes grew, not who made the wine; in Burgundy the producer matters as much as the site.21 The region is managed by 3,577 estates, 16 co-operatives and 266 trading companies, producing over 200 million bottles a year, half exported.17 High

Burgundy's price behaviour follows from the structure: tiny, fixed supply from the top climats; producer-direct allocation with no Place to absorb inventory; and a global collector base that grew sharply after 2010. It was the region that rose furthest in the 2020–22 boom and fell hardest afterwards.22 Med

IVChampagne

A house-led industry with a volume ceiling and a growing grower segment.

Champagne is organised around three producer types: the houses (négociant-manipulant), which buy grapes across the appellation and blend; the growers (récoltant-manipulant), who make wine from their own vines; and the co-operatives. Of the 266.1 million bottles shipped in 2025, almost 192 million came from houses and nearly 152 million went abroad.23 The appellation's harvest yields cap annual output at roughly 300 million bottles, so growth in the category comes from price, not volume, which is why every house with ambition has pushed into prestige cuvées.24 25 High

Concentration and the prestige tier

LVMH's Moët Hennessy division, Moët & Chandon, Veuve Clicquot, Dom Pérignon, Ruinart and Krug, accounted for roughly 22% of all Champagne shipments in 2025; Moët & Chandon alone is estimated at around 30 million bottles, close to a tenth of the appellation.23 28 Trade estimates put Dom Pérignon at around 5 million bottles and Krug at 425,000–500,000.28 Med The prestige sector was once defined by three brands, Cristal, Krug and Dom Pérignon, but has widened to include cuvées from Roederer, Pol Roger, Taittinger, Billecart-Salmon, Bollinger and Salon, and grand cru grape prices crossed €10 per kilo in 2022, making a luxury-priced cuvée an economic necessity for every house.29 Krug's founding principle, from 1843, was a multi-vintage blend rather than a vintage wine.30

The Champagne market is more polarised than ever: volume is down across the board while recognisable luxury brands and collector cuvées hold or grow.27 Grower Champagne, producers such as Egly-Ouriet, has moved upmarket over the past decade and now competes for the same export and fine-dining shelf space as the houses.24 31 Med

VItaly

The Super Tuscan revolt, and the Piedmont and Montalcino apex.

Italy introduced its DOC appellation system in the 1960s, modelled on the French AOC; Chianti's rules were widely criticised as restrictive, including a ban on 100% Sangiovese.32 The response came from the coast. Marchese Mario Incisa della Rocchetta had planted Cabernet Sauvignon at Tenuta San Guido in Bolgheri in 1944, reportedly from Lafite cuttings, and made Sassicaia for private use until his nephew Piero Antinori persuaded him to release the 1968 vintage commercially in 1971.33 34 Because no Bolgheri appellation existed, the wine was labelled vino da tavola.35 High

VIThe New World

A publicity stunt in 1976 ended France's monopoly on prestige.

On 24 May 1976 Steven Spurrier and Patricia Gallagher, who ran a Paris wine shop, staged a blind tasting of California against France before nine French judges. The New World demonstrated that a fine-wine market can be built without an 1855, a Place or a climat: critic, scarcity and direct allocation are sufficient. That is the same architecture Burgundy adopted after the grower revolution, and it produces the same result, high prices, thin liquidity, and provenance risk concentrated in the secondary market. VIIEvolution of the market

From merchant lists to an exchange, a Chinese boom, a fraud trial and a correction.

2000
Liv-ex, the London International Vintners Exchange, founded by stockbrokers James Miles and Justin Gibbs to bring stock-market transparency to a fragmented trade; the Fine Wine 100 launched December 2003, backdated to July 2001, calculated from mid-prices of real merchant transactions.44
2007–08
Hong Kong halves its 80% wine duty in 2007 and abolishes it in February 2008, after lobbying led by Henry Tang. Imports rise from $185 m (2007) to $858 m (2010); the territory overtakes London and New York as the largest fine-wine auction market by 2010 and introduces the first storage-accreditation scheme.45 46 47 48
2008–11
The Lafite era. Château Lafite Rothschild 2000, around £4,500 a case in 2007, trades above £14,000 by 2011; three bottles of 1869 Lafite sell at Sotheby's Hong Kong for HK$1.8 m each. An academic study finds Hong Kong auction prices carried a c.20% premium over other venues across 2008–14.49 47 50
2011
The Bordeaux-led boom peaks; prices turn negative from 2011 and Hong Kong auction results become more volatile.50 Jancis Robinson later calls the Hong Kong liberalisation arguably the single most powerful development in the modern history of wine.51
2013–14
Rudy Kurniawan, once among the most prominent US dealers, convicted in December 2013 of mail and wire fraud for manufacturing counterfeit rare wine at his California home between 2004 and 2012; sentenced in August 2014 to ten years, $20 m forfeiture and $28.4 m restitution, with losses near $30 m to seven victims including William Koch.52 53
2020–22
A second boom driven by low interest rates and pandemic-era wealth: Champagne, Burgundy and top Italian wines lead; the Champagne 50 nearly doubles.22 The broad Fine Wine 1000 peaks in October 2022.54
2023–25
Rising rates make cash yields competitive with a non-yielding, storage-cost-bearing asset; the broad market ends 2025 roughly 25–30% below its peak, with Burgundy the hardest hit and Bordeaux back near mid-2010s levels.54 55 56

The long view

Point-to-point over the ten years to the end of 2025, a span that includes both the boom and the correction, the Liv-ex 100 rose 34.1%, the Burgundy 100 around 106% and the Italy 100 around 61%, according to the Knight Frank Wealth Report 2026.13 Bordeaux underperformed all of them, and its share of exchange trade halved.12 Those are historical measurements, not current levels; the indices move monthly and should be read from Liv-ex directly. Provenance is the market's central risk. A 1982 Lafite with documented history was reported to sell for more than $12,000 a bottle against $500 for one of dubious heritage, and Hong Kong's climate made accredited storage a precondition of the auction boom.47 After Kurniawan, auction houses scrutinise old bottles repeatedly and wines sold directly by the producer command rising premiums as the only true guarantee of origin.58 Within the broad Liv-ex Fine Wine 1000, launch weights were Bordeaux 500 at 33%, Burgundy 150 at 28%, Italy 100 at 9% and Champagne 50 at only 3%, so a Champagne rally barely registers in the headline while a Bordeaux slump dominates it.57 High

Rough-diamond parallel: en primeur is a sight system. The producer sets the price, the intermediary is expected to take the allocation to preserve future access, and the merchant carries inventory risk into a secondary market it does not control. Both systems break when the secondary market trades below the primary price for long enough that intermediaries stop absorbing losses, the De Beers sight in 2024–25 and the Bordeaux négoce since the 2010 vintage are the same failure in different products.
