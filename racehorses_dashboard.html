Why Σ3

A racehorse is valued by opinion until it runs, and by its last race afterwards; there is no index, and the same yearling can be worth a tenth or ten times its purchase price within eighteen months. Exit is through a fixed calendar of auctions or a private sale, usually within months, but the asset eats, ages and can break while it waits. Custody is the heaviest on this site: training alone runs to tens of thousands a year, and most horses never earn it back. Title is clean, the registries are old and reliable, and syndication makes a share available to almost anyone.

ISupply and the sales

Fewer foals every year; more money for the best of them.

The Jockey Club projects a North American registered foal crop of 17,000 for 2026, 300 below the 2025 estimate of 17,300 and roughly half the crop of the mid-1980s. Mares bred fell from 34,121 in 2014 to 24,681 reported for 2025, and the number of covering stallions from over 1,100 to 740. Kentucky's 189 stallions covered 66% of all mares bred in North America.7 8 High

Against that, the yearling market has set a record every year since 2021. Keeneland September, the world's largest yearling sale, grossed $531.5 m in 2025 on 3,070 sold, up 24% on 2024's then-record $428 m; the average rose for the fifth consecutive year to $175,807 and the median to $80,000. Fifty-six yearlings made $1 m or more against a previous record of forty set in 2005; 120 buyers spent $1 m or more.1 2 High

Scarcity here is the reverse of Old Masters or stamps. The stock is renewed every year and the asset depreciates from the day it is bought: a yearling has one season of resale as a two-year-old, three or four seasons to race, then a residual value as a breeding animal or none at all. What is scarce is not the horse but the pedigree, which is why value has concentrated in the progeny of a dozen stallions while the crop as a whole shrinks. Hyp

The two headline sales tell the same story in different accents. At Keeneland the gains were broad: more million-dollar horses, more buyers spending a million, records in every book. At Newmarket turnover held flat on a smaller catalogue because a handful of buyers paid more per horse; one of every five guineas at Book 1 was Sheikh Mohammed's. Trade in Books 3 and 4, where most breeders sell, rose by single digits with clearance of 77%.3 5 6 High

Concentration of demand. A yearling market whose records depend on Godolphin, Coolmore and a few American partnerships is exposed to the decisions of perhaps ten people. The same has been said since the 1980s, and the buyers have changed (Maktoum, then Magnier, then Amo Racing and Resolute Bloodstock), but a holder exiting a top-end horse is selling into a room, not a market. IIIThe stallion economy

The fee a sire commands is the discount rate for the whole market.

A stallion is the only form of the asset that produces income rather than consuming it: a covering fee per mare, 100 to 250 mares a year, for ten to twenty seasons. For 2026 Dubawi (24) and Frankel (18) stand at £350,000, Sea The Stars at €300,000, Night Of Thunder at €200,000; in Kentucky Into Mischief, Gun Runner and Not This Time at $250,000. Yearlings by Frankel averaged 965,313 gns at Book 1 2026; Gun Runner's averaged near $1 m at Keeneland 2025 with seventeen at $1 m or more.4 9 11 High

A stallion is typically owned in forty shares, each carrying the right to one nomination a year, to use or sell; a syndication at a champion racehorse's retirement values the horse at a multiple of its expected first-year fees. The structure is the nearest the market comes to an income-producing security, and the risk is correspondingly specific: fertility, the quality of the first two crops of runners, and the fee cut that follows if they disappoint. Fee lists are revised every autumn and move by multiples: Vekoma from $35,000 to $100,000 in a year, Yaupon from $25,000 to $60,000; many sires fall as far in the other direction.11 Med

Not This Time ended 2025 as the second-leading sire in the United States without a single runner bred off a fee above $45,000. The stallion economy prices expectation; the racetrack prices outcome; and the gap between the two is where most of the money in this market is made and lost. Hyp9

The ownership economics are not in dispute. Roughly 8% of horses in training in North America earn enough in a year to cover their keep; owners collectively recoup around 40% of running costs from prize money, before the purchase price. In Britain the Racehorse Owners Association's own guidance is that a share should be treated as leisure spend. Returns, where they come, come from the residual: a colt that becomes a stallion, a filly that becomes a broodmare, or a horse sold on at the right moment.13 15 Med

Title is the market's strength. Every thoroughbred carries a registry passport (Weatherbys in Britain and Ireland, the Jockey Club in North America) with microchip and DNA parentage verification; ownership and syndicate registrations are public; transfer at auction is complete on the fall of the hammer, with the buyer carrying the risk from that moment. Provenance risk is low; integrity risk, medication and the regulatory record of a horse's connections, is the equivalent concern and sits under the Horseracing Integrity and Safety Authority in the United States and the BHA in Britain.13 High
