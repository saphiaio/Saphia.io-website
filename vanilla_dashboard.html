IThe cycle

Three price regimes in twelve years.

Indicative path, not a series. Anchors: $35–65 (2014), $500–800 (2018), $244.81 export unit value (2021), $250 floor removed (Apr/May 2023), $15–50 references (2026). Estimate1 2 8

Vines take about three years to bear, so plantings made into the 2018 boom matured exactly as high prices pushed food companies to substitutes. The market then fell for five consecutive years.2 Some distressed 2026 lots traded at $10–16/kg, and the trade references of $15/$25/$50 have never been formalised by decree.1

Discrepancy: one price-index vendor reports Madagascar averaging USD 200/kg in Q1 2026.9 This is inconsistent with every trade-house and origin source and appears to be a modelled retail or extract-equivalent series. Excluded from the analysis; retained in sources for transparency. Low

Widely repeated claims that Madagascar supplies "80% of the world's vanilla" refer to cured, export-grade beans in the international market, not green output.5 6 Madagascar's cured exports were 1,454 t (2019) and 1,724 t (2020), with the USA (810 t), France (339 t) and Germany (234 t) as principal destinations.7 The 41.9% and 80% figures are therefore both defensible but measure different things. High

Curing ratio: roughly 5–6 kg of green pods yield 1 kg cured, so a 3,000–3,500 t green crop implies c.550–700 t cured, but sources do not state their basis consistently. Treat all tonnages as basis-uncertain. Hyp

Each flower is pollinated by hand in a window of hours; cured beans pass through a multi-month Bourbon process (blanching, sweating, drying, conditioning). Around 65% of natural demand has historically been met from north-eastern Madagascar, where the price cycle sets local fortunes.5 6 At current farm-gate levels the trade's own question has inverted: not how high prices can go, but whether they are too low to keep growers in vanilla.6

Saffron parallel: both crops are labour-priced, not land-priced. The saffron page found stigma-picking labour to be the binding cost; here it is pollination and curing labour. Neither can mechanise, so both are exposed to rural wage inflation even in a price trough.

IVSubstitution & the synthetic ceiling

Natural bean extract is a rounding error in the flavour market.

RouteDescriptionPosition
Natural bean extractc.1% of global vanilla flavour volume; extraction yields 0.2–0.3% vanillin per kg of beansPremium; clean-label claims Med5 11
Synthetic vanillin (guaiacol, lignin)Bulk of the market; consistent chemistryCommodity High12
Bio-vanillin (eugenol / fermentation)Clove-oil oxidation at 65–80% conversion; fermentation routes can qualify as "natural" in some jurisdictionsNarrowing the gap with bean extract Med4 11

Reformulation is the asymmetric risk: sharp price increases have repeatedly caused manufacturers to accelerate substitution, permanently reducing bean demand, which is why the 2018 spike was self-destructive for the category.4 One market-research house values the vanilla-extract segment at USD 5.6 bn (2025); the figure is not reconcilable with bean tonnages and is Low confidence.13

Lab-grown diamond parallel: a synthetic with identical functional chemistry and a fraction of the cost, held back only by labelling ("natural") and by consumer perception of the 200-plus minor aroma compounds that vanillin alone lacks.13 The category's defence is provenance and sensory complexity, not price. Hyp
US retail for whole Grade A beans in August 2026 was roughly $97–225 per pound depending on origin, an order of magnitude above export references, illustrating where margin now sits in the chain.2
