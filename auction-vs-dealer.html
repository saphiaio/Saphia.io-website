Auction or dealer

When a holder sells, the counterparty is one of two kinds. An auction house is an agent: it takes the thing on consignment, sells it on a day it has fixed, in public, to the highest of whoever turns up, and charges both sides. A dealer is a principal: it names a price today, in private, and takes the thing onto its own book to sell later at a spread. Every one of the 51 market pages names a route that is one, the other, both with a threshold between them, or neither. This page reads them together.

IAgent or principal

The distinction is legal before it is commercial. An auction house never owns the lot. It acts for the consignor, charges the consignor a seller's commission on the hammer, charges the buyer a premium on top of the hammer, and if the lot fails to reach its reserve it goes back to the consignor, publicly unsold. A dealer owns what it sells. It buys from the holder at a price it names, holds the stock at its own risk and cost, and sells at a price it names later; the holder never sees the second price. 20 21

The sector pages give the two prices where they can. A watch sold to a dealer realises a discount of 15–30% to retail at once; the same watch on a marketplace takes two to eight weeks at a 6.5% commission; at auction it waits for the next sale and pays a buyer's premium that caps the bid. 22 A polished diamond sold privately realises "perhaps 40–60% of retail" through dealers, and goes to auction only if exceptional. 23 A yearling has no dealer route at all: the calendar at Keeneland and Tattersalls is the market, and a horse bought back by its vendor pays 2.5% for the privilege of not selling. 24

Three findings follow. The auction-led markets are the ones where the thing is unique and the price unknown until the room sets it: art, Old Masters, jewels, racehorses, cars, falcons, fossils. The dealer-led markets are the ones where a reference price exists and the dealer's job is to sit a spread around it: watches, polished diamonds, bullion, coins below the rarity line, oud, pu-erh. Between them, a dozen markets name a threshold in money below which the dealer takes it and above which the auction house does, and that threshold is where the holder's choice actually lies. The register sorts the 51 markets by these.

Derived observation

Add the two sides and the round trip through a major house on an ordinary lot is near 38% of hammer: 10% to the consignor's side and 28% to the buyer's, which the buyer deducts from the bid. A watch dealer's 15–30% discount is the same money paid once, at once, without a sale day. The auction is not cheaper than the dealer; it is a different allocation of the same spread, with the unsold-lot risk left with the seller. Hyp 20 22

VThree cases

Where the choice of counterparty, not the asset, set the outcome.

The unsold lotOld Masters, London, July 2026

The auction publishes the failure

Christie's evening sale made £38.9 m at 90% sold by lot, its highest sell-through in the category; Sotheby's the same week made £37.8 m at 78%, with ten lots unsold. An unsold Old Master is "burned": the trade saw the estimate, saw no bid, and will discount it for years. A dealer's unsold stock is invisible. This is the real price of the public price: the auction tells everyone what the thing is worth, including when the answer is nothing. 25

Watch: the share of lots withdrawn before sale, which houses use to keep the published rate up

The thresholdWatches

Three doors, three prices, one watch

The sector page gives the whole ladder. Sell to a dealer today: 15–30% below retail, cash now. List on Chrono24: 6.5% commission, two to eight weeks, buyers in 150 countries. Consign to a platform: 13–19%. Send to Phillips: wait for the Geneva or New York sale, pay a negotiable seller's commission, and let a 25%+ buyer's premium cap the bid. The holder of a steel Daytona takes door one or two; the holder of a Paul Newman takes door four. The reference price (dealer asking) exists continuously; the auction price exists twice a year and is the one that gets reported. 22

Watch: Chrono24's listing count and time-to-sale as the instant-offer market grows

ConvergenceSotheby's Marketplace, Rally, BaT

The houses become dealers, the dealers become auctions

Sotheby's now sells Birkins at a fixed price on a marketplace, paying the seller 80%, next to its own online auctions; it also brokers private sales in art that the sector page records as a growing share. In the other direction, Bring a Trailer and Collecting Cars turned the dealer's car into an online auction below $500,000, Reverb did the same for guitars, and Whisky Auctioneer runs 5,000 lots a month where a retailer once bought bottles over the counter. The two configurations in Section II are converging on a third: a continuous online auction with a fixed-price option, run by whoever has the audience. Hyp 31 29 26

VIThree patterns in the record

Pattern 1Uniqueness

Auction where the price is unknown, dealer where it is quoted

Every auction-led market in the register sells things with no two alike and no list price: paintings, yearlings, falcons, a T. rex. Every dealer-led market has a reference (Rapaport, Chrono24, Donghe, the LBMA price) and the dealer's business is the spread around it. The Who sets the price page found the same split from the other end: the calendar auction is a price regime, not just a venue.

Watch: any transaction-based index in a dealer-led market (diamonds, watches) that would let a house estimate without a room

Pattern 2Time against certainty

The dealer sells time; the auction sells the chance of a better price

Across the register the dealer's offer is immediate and the auction's is dated: Keeneland in September and November, Sotheby's fossils in July, Old Masters in July and December, the Riyadh falcon auction for three weeks in August. The holder who needs money this month has a dealer or nothing in roughly half the markets, and the Exit depth page records what that costs when the dealer is also the one who sold it.

Watch: auction-house advances and guarantees, which convert the dated price into an immediate one for a fee

Pattern 3Who bears the unsold

The seller at auction, the dealer in stock, the house never

An auction house carries no inventory risk unless it guarantees; it earns on both sides whether the lot makes high estimate or scrapes the reserve. A dealer's capital is tied up in whatever it bought and did not sell. The holder choosing between them is choosing who will be left holding the thing if the market turns between today and the sale day: at auction, themselves; with a dealer, the dealer, at a price that reflects it.

Watch: guarantee and third-party irrevocable-bid volumes at the major houses, where the house or a backer takes the dealer's risk for a share
