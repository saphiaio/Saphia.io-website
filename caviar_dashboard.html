Markets · Caviar

Caviar

Thirty years ago caviar was a wild Caspian fishery. Today it is an aquaculture product, more than nine-tenths of it farmed, a third or more of it Chinese, with supply now exceeding demand and wholesale prices falling, while the illegal wild trade persists on the Danube and in every market where a tin can be relabelled. This page covers species, the wild-to-farmed transition, production by country, trade, integrity and the Gulf's own experiment in desert aquaculture.

Farm economics follow from the biology. A decade of feeding before the first harvest means the capital cost sits in the female broodstock; meat from males and culled fish carries the farm until then, and the EU unit value of sturgeon meat has been sliding, from €8.56/kg in 2016 to €7.74/kg in 2020.3 Retail Beluga has been quoted at up to €600 per 100 g in CITES guidance, an order of magnitude above wholesale.14 Med

IIFrom wild fishery to aquaculture

The regulatory history is the market history.

1977
Peak recorded wild sturgeon catch, 31,800 t, almost all Caspian.1
1984
First FAO-recorded aquaculture harvest, 150 t.1
1991 onward
Collapse of the Soviet Union removes fisheries control on the Caspian; poaching and unregulated export follow.15
1998
All previously unlisted sturgeon and paddlefish species placed on CITES Appendix II; international trade requires permits.15 13
2000
CoP11 (Gigiri) recommends a universal caviar-labelling system to identify legal product in trade.15
2001–02
CITES suspends Caspian exports for nine months over poaching; trade resumes in March 2002 under annual quotas the range states must agree jointly.13 17
2002–03
World farmed sturgeon 4,100 t in 2002, half in Russia; China reports over 9,000 t in 2003 and the curve turns vertical.3
2004–05
CITES refuses most Caspian quotas for 2004; the US Fish and Wildlife Service lists beluga as threatened and bans wild beluga imports from 2005.17 16
2006–07
Romania is the first of several states to ban sturgeon fishing in the Danube and Black Sea; CITES again permits limited quotas in 2007 subject to stock surveys and regional strategy.1 13
2008
EU wildlife-trade regulation requires licensed processing and repackaging plants to keep records of caviar imported, exported, produced and stored.18
2015–18
Farmed sturgeon biomass peaks at c.129,600 t (2015) then settles around 102,000–115,000 t; global caviar output estimated at 380 t in 2018.5 1
2021–25
China alone reports 121,875 t of sturgeon; researchers describe a market in which supply has overtaken demand and wholesale prices have fallen.4
Diamond parallel: a wild resource whose scarcity created the luxury, replaced within two decades by a cultivated product with identical chemistry. The industry's response, origin labelling, species certification, a premium for "Caspian" or "Beluga" names, mirrors the natural-versus-lab-grown defence, with one difference: here the cultivated product is the legal one and the wild one is the contraband. The structural story is oversupply. Sturgeon planted in the early 2010s, in China above all, matured into a market whose demand had not grown at the same rate; the 2025 LCA study states plainly that supply surpasses demand and wholesale prices have decreased, even as caviar remains a luxury at retail.4 The margin has therefore migrated to branding, retail and hospitality, the Petrossian, Prunier and Kaluga Queen tins, rather than to the farm. Chinese producers that began as anonymous OEM suppliers to French, Swiss and American brands now sell under their own labels.6 Med

VIntegrity: labelling and fraud

The only luxury food with a mandatory international chain-of-custody label, and evidence that it is routinely defeated.

The CITES label

Every primary container of caviar in international trade must carry a non-reusable label with a standard species code, the source code (W wild, C captive-bred, F born in captivity to wild parents), the ISO country code of origin, the harvest year, the processing-plant registration code and the lot number; repackaging adds the plant code of the repackager and the re-export certificate number.14 18 Roe from non-listed fish is outside the system, and in the EU the word "caviar" is legally restricted to sturgeon and paddlefish.14 High

Bulgaria and Romania, J. Applied Ichthyology 201527High share mislabelled or unlabelled; sold as wild Danube; at least one "upgraded" to a pricier species12 High

Only DNA and stable-isotope analysis revealed the false declarations; the label alone did not.10 The Danube is the last European river with functional populations of beluga, Russian, stellate and sterlet sturgeon, and the study's authors note that a high proportion of poached product originates inside EU and accession states rather than in the developing world.10

Compliance reading: the CITES caviar label is structurally identical to a Kimberley Process certificate, it certifies the paperwork of the exporter, not the contents of the tin. The 2023 study is the caviar equivalent of a DNA-verified mine-of-origin audit, and it found half the sampled product non-compliant in the one region where wild stock still exists. For an importer, the control that works is laboratory verification of species and wild/farmed origin on a sampling basis, not label inspection. Med

The Abu Dhabi farm proved the technical case, a full-cycle sturgeon operation in a 50 °C climate, at exactly the moment Chinese volume began pushing wholesale prices down. The lesson for any Gulf agri-luxury venture, saffron included, is that closed-system production competes on cost against open-water Asian scale only where the brand, not the farm, captures the margin.
