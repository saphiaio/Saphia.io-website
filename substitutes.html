Synthetic and substitute pressure

A scarce asset stays scarce until something is made that does the same job. Cultured pearls did it to natural pearls a century ago; laboratory-grown diamonds are doing it now; cocoa-free chocolate and beanless coffee are trying. This page reads the record across the markets covered: which have a substitute, how far it has gone, where the price pressure lands, and what survives it.

IWhat a substitute does, and what it does not

A substitute is a product that is accepted as the same thing by most buyers at a lower price. It is not a fake: the cultured pearl is a pearl, the laboratory diamond is a diamond, vanillin is the molecule that makes vanilla taste of vanilla. What it removes is the scarcity premium on the part of the natural market that cannot be told apart from it without a laboratory, and that is the commercial middle of every market it enters.

What it does not remove is the premium on origin. A natural pearl still sells at Geneva with a report confirming it is natural; a Kashmir sapphire still sells on its origin report; a 5 ct natural D-flawless is priced as if the lab-grown market did not exist. The substitute converts the natural asset from a commodity, priced on grade, into a provenance asset, priced on proof. The grading report stops being a description and becomes the title.

The consequence for a holder is that the two tiers move in opposite directions and the line between them moves up over time. The sector pages for polished diamonds and coloured gemstones already record this split in their Scarcity lines ("Medium; large and coloured stones High", "High (top origins)"). This page sets out how far along the same path each market is.

A substitute removes
  • The scarcity premium on the commercial tier
  • Supply constraint: output follows capacity, not geology or harvest
  • The resale value of the natural commercial tier, once buyers cannot tell
  • The case for holding anything that is not provably natural
A substitute does not remove
  • The premium on proven origin at the top
  • Demand for the category; it usually expands it
  • The need for a laboratory, which it increases
  • The scarcity of the thing that cannot be made: a Burmese origin, a 1920s strand, a wild fish

IIIThe live case: diamonds

The pearl sequence compressed into a decade. Two independent US datasets agree that lab-grown stones are now the majority of new engagement rings; the wholesale price of the substitute has fallen by more than 90% while it took that share.

Where the pressure landed

The commercial natural stone, roughly half a carat to three carats, white, in the clarity grades that make up most engagement rings. The median lab-grown centre stone is now larger than the median natural one was in 2019, at a third of the price per carat. 1 3

What the substitute did to itself

Lab-grown prices followed manufacturing cost down, not natural prices. A product whose supply is capacity has no floor other than the marginal grower's cost, and the sector page records that there is no holding horizon at which it pays. 31

What natural did in response

Provenance infrastructure: De Beers' Tracr has registered more than 5 m rough stones, and GIA agreed to take a stake in May 2026. The same laboratories grade both products, so the certificate, not the stone, is now the asset being defended. 33

VThree patterns in the record

Pattern 1Price

The commercial tier goes to the substitute's cost curve

A lab-grown 1 ct stone fell from $1,800 to under $90 wholesale in eight years because its supply is capacity. 31 The natural commercial tier does not fall that far, but it loses its floor: it is priced against the substitute, less whatever buyers will pay to know it is natural.

Watch: the natural–lab price ratio in the commercial grades, now about four to one per carat 3

Pattern 2Proof

The certificate becomes the asset

Natural pearls, coloured stones and now diamonds all sell at the top with a laboratory report that says what they are and where they came from. The report is the title; the stone is the collateral. This raises the Provenance risk line on every sector page a substitute touches, because the same laboratories grade both.

Watch: laboratory disputes, undisclosed mixing, and who owns the provenance ledger

Pattern 3Speed

The sequence is getting shorter, except where origin was always the price

Pearls: 1893 to collapse by 1930, about 37 years. Diamonds: scale in 2018 to a US majority in 2025, about 7 years. Vanilla: effectively total within a generation of synthesis. Coloured gemstones: synthetic for 120 years and still confined to the commercial tier, because a ruby was never priced on being a ruby; it was priced on being Burmese.

Watch: whether cocoa and coffee behave like vanilla (an ingredient, priced on function) or like coffee's own specialty tier (priced on origin)

Derived observation

The markets most exposed to a substitute are those where the natural product is bought for a function that can be specified: a white stone of a given size and clarity, a round pearl of a given lustre, a flavour. The markets least exposed are those where the product is bought for where it came from or who made it. The sector pages' Scarcity lines already split along this seam; a substitute does not create the split, it finds it. Hyp

VIIndicators to monitor

  • Lab-grown share of engagement rings outside the United States, where neither of the two series cited here reaches.
  • Lab-grown adoption in diamond categories other than engagement rings; BriteCo reports it expanding to earrings, bands and pendants 3.
  • Any commercial lab-grown coloured stone marketed with the certification and branding that took lab-grown diamonds from 6% to a majority; the sapphire and ruby mid-market is the exposed tier.
  • Cocoa-free capacity against world grindings; the 15,000 t Planet A target and Voyage's Cargill distribution are the two numbers to track 8 9.
  • Whether beanless products are sold as coffee or as blends; the 50:50 format is a sign the substitute is not yet accepted on its own.
  • Fermentation-derived "natural" vanillin taking label share from pod extract, which would remove the last premium use of the bean 5.
  • The natural-pearl and natural-diamond auction results, which are the leading indicator of where the top of the natural diamond market settles.
