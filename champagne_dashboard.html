Why Σ2

Champagne is the most legible of the collectible drinks. Liv-ex publishes a daily index of fifty prestige cuvées and mid-prices for each; bottles are held in bond in a merchant's warehouse with the owner's name on the record; exit is to the merchant or through the exchange in weeks; and the asset is a branded, dated, sealed object whose authenticity is rarely in question. The one material limit is the one Fine Wine carries: transaction costs, 10% or more on each trade and storage of a few pounds a case a year, which on a product that has just fallen a third is the whole return of several years. The houses also set release prices and yields, so the holder's scarcity is partly the producer's decision.

IShipments and the yield lever

The only wine region that sets its own supply each August.

Shipments were 266.1 m bottles in 2025, down 2.1% on 2024's 271 m and well below the 326 m of 2022; France took 114 m (−3.6%), exports 152 m (−0.9%). Revenues were about €5.6 bn. The first half of 2026 recovered 1.2% to 107.1 m bottles, driven by export. Prestige cuvées, the part of the market that trades, fell much faster: exports down 17% to 7 m bottles, 4.6% of the total against 5.5%; in Britain 480,000 bottles against a ten-year average of 820,000.1 3 4 5 High

The Comité Champagne, the joint body of growers and houses, fixes the marketable yield each year before harvest. For 2026 it is 8,800 kg per hectare, roughly 250 m bottles, the lowest since the 8,000 kg of 2020. The mechanism matches supply to the stock in cellar, which runs to several years of sales, and is the structural reason Champagne corrections are shallower and shorter than Burgundy's.3 High

Liv-ex's description is "correction to consolidation": the index fell 33.1% in 35 months, has risen 1.7% since, and the components are split. Bordeaux Index calls the outlook "flat"; iDealwine reports auction value up 21% on volume up 4%. The houses' own response, cutting release prices by a sixth after raising them by a sixth, is the clearest signal that the 2020–22 pricing was a cycle rather than a level.1 2 6 High

The Champagne 50 is dominated by vintage releases from the large houses, which is why it has held better than non-vintage shipments and why the index can stabilise while the region's volumes fall. A holder is exposed to the house's release policy twice: a cut in the next release reprices the back vintages, and a late-disgorged or library release from the house's own cellar competes directly with the secondary market.1 8 Med

IVHouses and the appellation

Three producer types, a volume ceiling and the dozen houses that set the prestige tier.

Champagne is organised around three producer types: the houses (négociant-manipulant), which buy grapes across the appellation and blend; the growers (récoltant-manipulant), who make wine from their own vines; and the co-operatives. Of the 266.1 million bottles shipped in 2025, almost 192 million came from houses and nearly 152 million went abroad.10 The appellation's harvest yields cap annual output at roughly 300 million bottles, so growth in the category comes from price, not volume, which is why every house with ambition has pushed into prestige cuvées.11 12 High

The 2024 fall was the sharp one: 271.4 m bottles, −9.2%, with France at 118.2 m (−7.2%) and exports 153.2 m (−10.8%), after producers cut the harvest in July when first-half sales fell more than 15%. In 2025 the top export markets were the United States (26.5 m bottles), the United Kingdom (22.7 m) and Japan (13.3 m).13 14 High

Concentration and the prestige tier

LVMH's Moët Hennessy division, Moët & Chandon, Veuve Clicquot, Dom Pérignon, Ruinart and Krug, accounted for roughly 22% of all Champagne shipments in 2025; Moët & Chandon alone is estimated at around 30 million bottles, close to a tenth of the appellation.10 15 Trade estimates put Dom Pérignon at around 5 million bottles and Krug at 425,000–500,000.15 Med The prestige sector was once defined by three brands, Cristal, Krug and Dom Pérignon, but has widened to include cuvées from Roederer, Pol Roger, Taittinger, Billecart-Salmon, Bollinger and Salon, and grand cru grape prices crossed €10 per kilo in 2022, making a luxury-priced cuvée an economic necessity for every house.16 Krug's founding principle, from 1843, was a multi-vintage blend rather than a vintage wine.17

The Champagne market is more polarised than ever: volume is down across the board while recognisable luxury brands and collector cuvées hold or grow.14 Grower Champagne, producers such as Egly-Ouriet, has moved upmarket over the past decade and now competes for the same export and fine-dining shelf space as the houses.11 18 Med

VThe economics of holding

Low custody, material friction, a product that is drunk.

ItemOrder of costNote
Storage in bond£10–20 a case a yearIncludes insurance at replacement value in most merchants' schemes Med
Merchant margin on sale10–15% of valueOr the exchange spread plus the merchant's fee
Auction commissionSeller's 10–15%, buyer's 20–25%Older vintages and formats only
Duty and VATOn removal from bondPayable only if the bottles leave the system
ConditionFill level, label, capsule, disgorgement datePrestige cuvées are checked on receipt into bond; a case that has left bond is repriced on re-entry

The asset is consumed, which is the structural difference from every other collectible: the stock of Krug 2008 falls every year by what is drunk, and the house does not reissue it (library releases excepted). That drawdown is the long-run case for back vintages and the reason the index is split between stable back vintages and softening new ones. Provenance is simple for anything bought on release and kept in bond; for older bottles outside the system, storage history is unknowable and the market discounts accordingly.2 Med
