Why Σ3

Valuation is by expert appraisal against a thin but well-documented record: dealer price guides, the Burst serial registry, Tarisio's Cozio archive of every known Cremonese instrument. Exit for a guitar is through a handful of dealers and two or three auction houses with regular sales, in weeks to months; for a Stradivari it is a private placement through a London or New York dealer to a foundation or a patron, which can take a year. Custody is simple but not free: humidity control, insurance, and for bowed instruments regular playing and maintenance. The specific risk is originality: a refinish, a changed pickup or a re-graduated top can halve the price, and the market's certificate is a dealer's letter, not a laboratory report.

Two markets that share a logic rather than a buyer. Golden-era American guitars are a collector market with a deep middle, a published price guide and a new record every few years; Cremonese violins are a patron market of a few hundred instruments, most owned by foundations and lent to players. This page covers the record book for each, what originality means and costs, how the trade is structured, the risks, and what to watch. No prices are carried as live values.

Three prices are being set here, and only one is a market. Guitars sold on merit have a price guide, a serial registry and enough transactions a year for a dealer to quote; the Kato Burst is a 3.6× move on the 2020 comparable, but both are prints in a continuous series. Celebrity guitars are memorabilia, priced on the owner and the moment, and tell a holder of an ordinary Burst nothing. Cremonese instruments trade a few times a year, almost always privately, and the Joachim-Ma's failure to reach its low estimate in a well-publicised sale is as informative as the number.1 2 4 Med

The same guitar is worth two or three different sums depending on what has been touched.

Dealer retail estimates; auction hammer prices run below retail and above wholesale. Med

For bowed instruments the equivalent variables are the condition of the original varnish, whether the top has been re-graduated or patched, the authenticity of the scroll, and the dendrochronology of the spruce; a certificate from a recognised expert (historically Hill, Beare, Machold; now Tarisio, Florian Leonhard, Ingles & Hayday, Reuning) is the title document in all but name. Attribution moves value by the same orders of magnitude as in Old Masters: "Stradivari", "workshop of", "attributed to" and "after" are different assets.2 6 Med

The guitar market's unusual advantage is a public registry. The Burst Serial Registry and the dealer community track individual instruments by serial number across decades of ownership, which is closer to a stud book than to anything in the art market. It does not stop forgery, but it makes a newly surfaced "original" guitar with no history a known risk rather than an unknown one.1 3 Hyp

The German trade estimates its own vintage-guitar market at €45 m a year growing 7%, with 8–12% price gains in 2026; the US market is several times larger. The fine-string market has no published aggregate; Tarisio's archive records some 650 surviving Stradivari instruments and about 150 by Guarneri del Gesù, of which a minority are in private hands and fewer still available.6 7 Med

Holding costs are low: insurance at agreed value through specialist underwriters, a case and a humidifier, and for a violin an annual check by a luthier. Title passes with possession and the invoice; there is no registry of ownership, so provenance is the dealer chain and, for guitars, the serial registry's record of who has reported the instrument.
