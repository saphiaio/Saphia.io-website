Why Σ4

Catalogue prices exist for every stamp ever issued, which gives the illusion of a benchmark; actual realisations for all but the rarest items run at a fraction of catalogue and fall as collectors die faster than they are replaced. Exit for a serious collection means a specialist auction, months of preparation and 15–25% in fees, and a weak result is common. Custody is trivial; the risks are condition, regumming and forgery, which only a certificate from an expert committee controls.

The oldest mass collecting hobby and the one whose demographics are most against it. Every stamp has a catalogue number and a printed price, every major rarity a published census, and yet nobody can say what the market is worth because almost all of it changes hands privately or not at all. This page covers what is known of size and trend, the record book, the condition and expertising system that decides value, the trade's structure, and what to watch. ISize and cycle

No reliable total exists; the direction is clearer than the size.

Trade estimates of the global philatelic market range from $5 bn to $10 bn a year, figures that originate with dealers and are repeated without a method; ΣΟΦΙΑ carries none of them. What can be observed: the number of collectors in Western countries has fallen for thirty years as the hobby's post-war generation dies; auction houses report rising totals on fewer, better lots; and Chinese material, from the 1878 Large Dragons to the 1968 "Whole Country is Red" error, has been the one area of sustained price growth, driven by mainland buyers through Hong Kong.4 Low for any total; Med for the direction.

The estate problem. Most inherited collections are accumulations of common material whose catalogue value runs to tens of thousands and whose auction value is a few hundred pounds. The catalogue is a dealer's selling price for a perfect example, not a bid. A holder should expect 10–30% of catalogue for ordinary material and should treat any valuation quoted "at catalogue" as meaningless. Med

The 2021 sale of the 1c Magenta is the market's most informative result. A unique object with a century of fame, offered by a respected house with a $10–15 m estimate, drew a hammer of $7 m from the trade, not a collector, and was then sold on in £100 fractions to the public. A rarity sets a floor on interest, not a ceiling on price.3 High

Parallel to coins: the expertising certificate is the stamp world's slab, but it is an opinion on a loose object, not a sealed holder, and committees have revised opinions. Unlike coins, no numeric grading scale has gained universal acceptance, so two certificates can describe the same stamp differently. Hyp
