About

Intelligence on the world's scarce assets. Independent, cited research on markets that are hard to understand, compare and value, read with one method.

Why this exists

A common language for markets that do not have one

Not all wealth sits in accounts. A great deal of it sits in cellars, safes, humidors and drawers, and it is changing hands faster than at any time in history.

Gold has an exchange, a daily fix and a century of data. Saffron has none of these. Between them lie some fifty markets with their own vocabularies, their own gatekeepers and no shared way of being read. Information is held by the specialist, prices are discovered in rooms the holder is not in, and the gap between the number in the catalogue and the number a buyer will pay is rarely written down.

The questions are the same whoever asks them. What is this, and can that be proved? How scarce is it, really? Who controls supply? Who buys, where, and at what discount? What does it cost to hold, and how long to sell? The dealer knows the answers; the heir, and often the adviser, does not know the questions. Saphia exists to narrow that gap.

Who it serves

Those responsible for understanding value

The adviser asked to take a view on a client's cellar, watch collection or parcel of coloured stones, and expected to challenge the specialist's number with something better than instinct. The family office comparing the practicality of holding gold, wine and art on one scale rather than on three specialists' terms.

The heir who inherits four hundred bottles of Bordeaux, a sapphire with a Burmese certificate and a painting whose artist they have to look up, and needs to know what to keep before deciding what to sell. The collector deciding what to add.

It is not advice, and it does not replace the specialist. It is the briefing you would want before speaking to one.

How it is read

Seven questions, one grade

Every market is read in the same order, to the same evidence rules: every figure cited and dated, no live prices, discrepancies shown rather than resolved, inference marked as inference. Four structural ratings, never combined into a score, and one opinion: a Σ grade for how practical the asset is to own and exit, stated separately from any view on return.
