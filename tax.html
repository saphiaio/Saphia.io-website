Tax and legal wrappers

Two questions sit under every market page and are rarely asked together: what does the holder legally own, and what does the state take when it is sold? A bar in a vault, a case in bond, one of forty shares in a stallion, a Reg A security in a painting and a token on a ledger are five different legal things, and the tax on the way out is set by that legal form as much as by the asset. This page reads the wrappers and the tax points across all 51 markets.

The UK is the reference regime because the market briefs are written from London; the US, Germany, France, Switzerland and the trading hubs are read against it in Section IV.

IForm before tax

Tax law does not know what a scarce asset is. It knows a chattel (tangible movable property), a wasting asset (a chattel with a predictable life under fifty years), legal tender, a security, trading stock, livestock and land, and it taxes each differently. A UK holder who sells a vintage Patek Philippe at a gain pays no capital gains tax, because a watch is machinery and machinery is a wasting asset; the same holder selling a bottle of 1960s Macallan at the same gain pays it, because spirits do not deteriorate and the bottle is a non-wasting chattel. 1 2 A US holder pays a maximum federal rate of 28% on either, because both are "collectibles", and pays the same on a gold ETF, because the trust that holds the metal is looked through to the metal. 3 4

The wrapper changes the form. A case of wine in a bonded warehouse is a chattel on which duty and VAT have not yet been charged; the same case at home has paid both and can no longer be sold into the professional market. 20 A stallion share is a contractual interest in a co-owned animal; a Masterworks share is a security issued by a Delaware LLC; a unit in the Sprott uranium trust is an interest in a Canadian trust that a US holder must elect to treat as a qualified electing fund or face punitive rules. 21 22 5 The Access page asked what each wrapper owns; this page asks what the law calls it and what that costs on exit.

Three findings run through the register. First, the physical assets with the strongest liquidity on the sector pages, bullion and wine, are also the ones with the most generous reliefs in the UK, and the reliefs are old: legal-tender coins, investment-gold VAT exemption, the wasting-asset rule and the bond. Second, the assets this site rates hardest to exit are mostly held in forms the tax law treats as trading stock or a farm, where income tax rather than capital gains tax applies and the question is a business one. Third, the hubs the Sanctions page found the trade moving to, Dubai, Hong Kong, Singapore and Geneva, are hubs partly because they tax none of this.

Derived observation

The hubs are not tax havens for scarce assets by accident. Hong Kong's wine duty went to zero in 2008 and the city became the auction centre for Asia within three years; Geneva's freeport holds more art than any museum because a work can be bought, stored and sold inside it without crossing a tax point; Dubai's reverse charge is what lets rough move through the DMCC without 5% leaking at each hand. The Sanctions page found the trade moving to the hubs when origin is restricted; this page finds the same movement when the home regime taxes the exit. Hyp 23 32 33

VThree cases

Where the legal form, not the asset, decided what the holder kept.

Wasting assetUK, cars and watches v spirits

Fifty years is the line

HMRC's manual exempts any chattel with a predictable useful life under fifty years and deems all machinery wasting, naming clocks, watches and motor cars. A Ferrari sold at Monterey or a Nautilus sold on Chrono24 by a UK resident carries no capital gains tax, however large the gain. A bottle of Macallan or a case of vintage port is a non-wasting chattel and taxed above £6,000. The cask firms of 2019–25 marketed casks as wasting assets; HMRC's published position is that it depends on the facts and is not to be assumed, and the administrators of the three insolvent firms inherited the question. 2 12 26

Watch: any HMRC ruling or tribunal case on cask whisky and the wasting-asset rule

Look-throughUS, the gold ETF

A share taxed as a bar

The largest physically backed gold ETFs are grantor trusts; the US holder is treated as owning a share of the metal, so a long-term gain is a collectibles gain at up to 28%, not the 20% that applies to a share. The fund's prospectus says so. A futures-based commodity fund is taxed instead under Section 1256 at 60% long and 40% short, whatever the holding period, and issues a K-1. Two gold exposures, two rates, and the difference is the wrapper's legal form, not the metal. 4 14

Watch: proposals to reclassify physically backed ETFs out of the collectibles rate, raised and dropped in several Congresses

Bond and freeportWine, whisky, art

The tax point is a door, and the trade stays inside

Wine in a UK bonded warehouse has paid neither duty nor VAT; it trades between bonded accounts on Liv-ex without either, and the sector page records that wine which leaves bond "loses verifiable provenance and resells at a discount". The same logic built the freeports: Geneva's is estimated to hold over a million works, and Singapore's Le Freeport opened in 2010 to the same design. The holder who never takes delivery never crosses the tax point; the holder who does pays it and cannot easily come back. Low on the Geneva figure. 20 32 34

Watch: EU anti-money-laundering rules extending customer-due-diligence to freeport operators and art dealers above €10,000

VIThree patterns in the record

Pattern 1Classification

The rate follows the noun

Across the register the asset's economics barely move the tax; its legal category does. A UK watch and a UK handbag are both luxury goods resold through the same houses; one is exempt and the other is not. A US gold bar and a US gold ETF are both 28%; a US gold-miner share is 20%. The holder's first question is which noun the law uses, and the sector pages' Instruments blocks are where the answer starts.

Watch: HMRC manual updates to the wasting-asset list; US Treasury guidance on tokenised metals

Pattern 2Suspension

Where the trade is deep, the tax is deferred

Bond, freeport, reverse charge and the professional exchange are all the same device: the state agrees not to collect at each hand so long as the goods stay in the system. The markets this site rates most liquid among physical assets, bullion, wine, whisky in bond, Antwerp and Dubai rough, run inside such systems. The markets without one, saffron, oud, cigars, fossils, pay the tax on each crossing and the sector pages record the thinness.

Watch: the UK's April 2026 restriction of agricultural and business property relief, which touches groves, orchards, herds and studs

Pattern 3Residence

Germany's year, Switzerland's nothing, the hubs' nothing

A German resident who holds any chattel for twelve months sells it free of tax; a Swiss resident always does; a Hong Kong, Singapore or Dubai resident does too. The UK reliefs are narrower and older and the US has none. The wrappers that cross borders, Reg A shares, Canadian trusts, Jersey plcs, offshore wine funds, add a second regime to the first and a set of elections (QEF, reporting fund) that a retail holder rarely makes.

Watch: the UK's replacement of the non-dom regime from April 2025 and its effect on London as a place to hold chattels

VIIIndicators to monitor

  • HMRC guidance or a First-tier Tribunal decision on whether maturing whisky in cask is a wasting asset; the insolvency reports of the 2025 cask firms 12 26.
  • The UK's April 2026 cap on 100% agricultural and business property relief at £1m, and the effect on studs, groves and herds that the register lists as land or livestock 16.
  • Any change to the US collectibles rate or to the grantor-trust treatment of physically backed metal and uranium vehicles 3 4.
  • EU anti-money-laundering rules applied to freeport operators and art dealers, and whether volume moves between Geneva, Singapore and Delaware 34.
  • France's flat-tax rates on precious metals and collectibles, which have moved in successive finance laws 6.
  • Whether the SEC or Treasury classifies tokenised gold and diamond vehicles as securities, commodities or the underlying collectible for tax 35.
