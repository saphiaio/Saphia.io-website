A $60 billion market that returned to growth in 2025 after two years of contraction, carried by the very top: a single Klimt portrait accounted for a fifth of the largest auction week since 2021, and every one of the year's ten most expensive lots was sold in New York. This page covers the market's size and structure, the 2025 recovery, regional shares, the auction record book and the structural shifts the benchmark report identifies. Both channels grew: dealers by 2% to $34.8 bn and public auctions by 9% to $20.7 bn, while reported private auction sales fell 4% to just under $4.2 bn as sellers returned to the saleroom.2 3 UBS frames the adjustment against the "Great Wealth Transfer", more than $83 trillion to pass between generations, shifting collecting toward women and younger buyers.1 High, these are the report's own estimates; all values are Arts Economics' constructs from surveys and auction data, not observed totals.

Profitability diverged from sales: despite growth, 38% of dealers and 40% of mid-tier auction houses reported lower profits than the previous year; in the $250,000–500,000 dealer segment, 45% saw margins fall on rising sales. Costs, fairs, travel, regulation, shipping, rose faster than turnover.3 High

Dealer sentiment for 2026 improved markedly, 43% expected turnover to rise, up from 33% a year earlier, with 38% expecting stability, on the strength of late-2025 fairs and auctions and record highs at the top.4 High

Diamond parallel: the art market is the diamond market with the certificate removed. Value rests on attribution, provenance and condition established by experts rather than by a laboratory; the auction houses play the role of the bourse; and the "fresh to market single-owner collection" is the equivalent of an untouched parcel of rough. The difference is liquidity, a Klimt trades once a generation, a D-flawless every week. Hyp
